Fatherhood Diaries: a thousand questions

The children are growing up too fast. I’m glad they are healthy and developing as expected, but I’m just not ready. My oldest, Johnny, is turning nine today. Like Schrödinger’s cat, he is both a child and a pre-teen. He sometimes exhibits behaviours of such intensity and disproportionality only expected of teenagers, while other times he is going out of his way to kiss and hug me, still craving my attention in that sweet, childish, nurturing kind of way.

Johnny has many of my attributes and idiosyncrasies. Seeing part of me in him makes me feel uneasy. I have a lot of shortcomings. But what I absolutely love is that he is also a near carbon copy of Sunshine. Huge brain engine, similar stubbornness, equal natural curiosity coupled with unprecedented geekiness. In him, I see Sunshine—as Rousseau would put it—without society’s chains. Raw, pure, genuine, lovable.

I adore how innocent he is. How everything is new and potentially subject to scrutiny. How he picks up on things independently. How he is mimicking and questioning behaviours, mannerisms, and opinions. Seeing him navigating early life reminds me that social conventions are not innate; they are tacit collective agreements, sometimes spanning generations, shaped by the shared history of particular groups of people. I guess that’s what humans do: they create and sustain bonds, being social animals and all that.

Back to the innocence. The other day he questioned Santa Claus’ existence. He would tentatively accept that Santa might indeed be real, but seriously questioned the plausibility of the flying reindeer pulling his sleigh. How absolutely cute. I melted and wanted to hug and kiss him, but instead I maintained a poker face and responded with all the seriousness fitting a young gentleman’s question.

Johnny is now reading the books that both Sunshine and I were reading at his age. Seeing him adopt the very habit that unites us warms my heart. Being rather solitary beings, we grew to attach great value to reading, using books as a shelter from the world, and occasionally as a coping mechanism. The ability to travel to new places and entertain novel—even heretical—ideas while on the couch is priceless.

Occasionally I get tired and fed up, requiring a bit of space and silence. After all, Johnny utters the phrase “Dad, can I tell you something?" or “Dad, I have a question for you” about a thousand times a day, even while I’m already in the middle of a conversation with him. Yet, even when I’m frustrated, I realise that there is a limit to how many times he will address me this way or attach substantial weight to my answers. I know that I will dearly miss these finite encounters, and that I will (all too) soon reminisce about the bygone era when the kids were little.

Despina is younger, still fitting comfortably within the definition of a preschooler. She is slowly learning how to navigate the world and find her place in it. She is extremely cute, and everyone is mesmerised by her. They are all too eager to obey her every whim, to cater to her every need or desire, however irrational. This is something we are trying to manage, not always successfully. My favourite interaction with her is when I come home tired, or when I am pissed or generally consumed by one negative feeling or another, and she runs towards me, hands open up in the air, ready to jump and get a big hug. My mood instantly changes as if with a flip of a switch.

I’m very proud of them. Can we do better as parents? Absolutely. I’m bringing way too much stress home from work, we are both spending more time on our devices than we should, we are not always as present as would be ideal, and generally we could be better role models. But life is about embracing our imperfections and making the best of that situation.

Unfortunately, I also came to grips with a harsh truth: that the version of a father I am today and the effort I put into it reflects the maximum of my current abilities. I just don’t have the physical energy or the mental stamina for anything additional. This worries me: it may not be enough at some point. What gives me solace is the knowledge that adversities are also coupled with the development of coping mechanisms, what we call resilience. So I choose to place faith in the abilities of my future self to be resilient and rise to the occasion if that is necessary.

I’m told these are the “easy years.” Everyone I know raising teenagers tells me to enjoy the moment because from early teenhood onwards it’s an uphill battle, at least in relation to the complexities of the issues that need to be addressed, and the risks facing the children.

For now, though, Johnny is nine, and I’m still his beloved father who is there to answer his seemingly infinite questions. I’m not quite ready for that change.
