On reading Ulysses at forty

Dear future self,

Back in July 2026, in the month of your fortieth birthday, you suddenly became conscious of the time gone by. You felt that time was running out, and consequently, that it was high time to pursue things you wanted to achieve or experience; pursuits that were deprioritised as you were trying to juggle a demanding job, two children and a social life. The list included both intellectual and physical pursuits: to run a marathon (achieved a few months prior), to bicycle 200km (failed a few years prior when, at the 180th km, you were knocked off your bicycle by the support car), and—the topic of this entry—to read James Joyce’s Ulysses. You had started this book a few times already, but always gave up on it as it was incomprehensible to you. Yet, many people whose intellect you admire kept referring to Ulysses as the greatest book of all time, and to James Joyce as the most brilliant of authors, certainly the best among the English speakers.

It was then that you decided it was time to tackle the beast that is James Joyce’s Ulysses. After all, those well-read intellectuals cannot all be wrong. You researched the book, and realised that it could not be read on its own. In the true geeky fashion that characterises you, you started going through secondary literature in order to grasp the basics, even before starting the book. And when you did, you read commentary on each chapter prior to reading it, as well as all endnotes from the Oxford Classics edition you had on your bookshelf since your twenties. Even with the supporting documentation, the YouTube videos, the CliffsNotes and whatnot, the book was still difficult to navigate. You had trouble following the author’s train of thought and the storyline. Although your first instinct was to feel inadequate as a reader, you got past that, thinking that if you could read and enjoy the most famous of classic literature, the fact that you did not easily understand Joyce’s Ulysses had more to do with him than with you.

As the book progressed, it became obvious to you that Joyce was truly brilliant. His ability to use different styles and voices to write his magnum opus, with each chapter as if written by a different author, and with an obsessive attention to detail in the story, in his descriptions, and in the depth—or lack thereof—of his characters, coupled with his witty remarks, certainly classifies him as a literary genius. But that’s it. This is what Joyce was interested in doing: to demonstrate his brilliance to the elites of his era. I came to visualise Joyce’s publication of Ulysses as follows. I imagine a small room of the intellectual pillars of his time—inevitably all men—crowded around a tiny square table, discussing this and that, being loud and entitled. Suddenly, Joyce enters the room, they quiet down, and make room for him to approach the table. Without talking to them, he unzips his pants, takes out his instrument, which is larger than any mortal previously carried, and laid it on the table with a bang. The rest were struck with awe, admiration and jealousy. He zipped his trousers, said “gentlemen” while tipping his hat, turned around and left. I came away convinced that this was his objective in writing and publishing Ulysses. He wanted to show his peers that he had the biggest cock of them all. He was not interested in writing a good novel. The novel itself is merely a means of making his big statement: that he is better than them in every possible way.

I write this to tell you that you’ve read this novel once and it is enough. We know each other fairly well by now, to foresee that at some point in the future, possibly when the children are gone, you will want to read this book again, trying to prove to yourself that you are not a lesser person, a lesser reader, a lesser intellectual and whatnot. Don’t. It is not about you, and the book’s purpose was not to be easily readable or even enjoyable. Read something else; you don’t have much time left—and that was already obvious when you were forty and the kids were little, uncomplicated, and fascinated by what you had to say.

You are okay,
Your forty-year-old self
