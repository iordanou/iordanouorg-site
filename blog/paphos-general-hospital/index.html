Urgent, but not that urgent

I’m prefacing this by saying that all concerned are well, so no need for get-well wishes and other remarks of that sort. In this post, I will give you a first-hand account of the events that unfolded during our summer holidays in Latsi, when a member of our family needed urgent medical care.

It was Saturday noon when a medical emergency arose. We called our personal doctor informing her of the urgent situation and asking for her advice. The doctor replied via text only to send the name of a specialist, whom we could obviously not contact as it was a Saturday, so not particularly helpful. Off to the nearby hospital then, the one in Polis Chrysochous. Ten minutes’ drive, fifteen minutes to register and pay the €10 fee, three minutes for the examination, only to be told that we must go to the General Hospital in Paphos, some fifty minutes away, in order to rule out one—life-altering—condition. Alas, we were told that while urgent, it was not that urgent, so “do not speed”—thank you very much. Off we went then to Paphos General Hospital, again waiting some half an hour for triage. We were prioritised and given a bed almost immediately, seeing as it was indeed urgent. In the meantime, doctors and nurses were coming and going, engaging in loud commentary indicative of the fact that they did not really mind being overheard. More surprising to me was their general dismissive demeanour, especially towards non-Greek speakers (not us).

While the triaging led to us being admitted fairly quickly, we nonetheless waited for a couple of hours until the radiologist could slot us in. He examined us and was indeed quite polite, and off we went back to the A&E. Nearly an hour passed until his report was received (four lines), during which time the A&E doctor had us sitting in the corridor. Both of us knew that the radiologist’s examination didn’t yield a worrying outcome, yet no action was taken until the report was received. While in the corridor we had a full view of others in beds, in pain, some evidently having the worst day of their lives, all without privacy, and some without compassion. Characteristic was the case of an old man, a third-country national if it matters to you, in pain having broken his shoulder, with the medical staff being around him and rather than immediately administering a painkiller, discussing among them in Greek how he looked like someone they knew. Surreal.

The A&E doctor finally called a specialist and read him the report over the phone (!). The specialist—who was on duty at the very same hospital—told the A&E doctor that it might be one thing or the other and that, in any case, we had to come back on Monday to see him, though he could just see us then and there and spare us another fifty minute drive and another one of our holidays gone. In the meantime, he told us to urgently head to the A&E if a specific event occurred, and he just shrugged when I told him, once more, that we are fifty minutes away from the hospital and we would not be able to make it in due time if there was an emergency. Their exchanges on whether there was a café open that could deliver coffee, seemed invariably more important than we were.

In the meantime, the doctor who seemed in charge of the A&E, the one who was also in charge of our case, was on an absolute power trip, yelling at his coworkers, sometimes as a joke and other times due to frustration, overall acting like a spoiled toddler. Note by the way that the situation in the A&E was far from unmanageable—it was nowhere near as busy as an average weekend in Nicosia General Hospital.

We made our way back to Latsi, and spent Saturday evening and Sunday there, and woke up early on Monday to head to Paphos. The drive is scenic; it makes perfect sense that people from the district of Paphos have a heightened sense of localism, as their part of Cyprus is by far the prettiest—and the reason why we visit every year. Long story short, we made our way to the General Hospital, and saw the specialist doctor almost immediately. The visit was overall pleasant, and the specialist doctor was thorough and elaborate, while transparent as to the limit of his knowledge—he literally used ChatGPT for his diagnosis.

All in all, another unpleasant experience in Cyprus’ public hospitals, with nurses being overall accommodating and polite, and (A&E) doctors being arrogant and entitled, all at the expense of the patients paying their salaries.
