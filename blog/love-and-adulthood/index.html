Love and… adulthood

I am triggered when people start a sentence with “I feel”. The voice inside me is screaming: “Why would I care about what you feel? I only care about what you think, and only if it makes sense to me. Feelings cannot be given the same credence as rational thought, as they cannot withstand the same level of scrutiny.” Yet, much of what I believe was once a “mere” feeling; one that I rationalised, assessed and eventually endorsed as a belief. So maybe I should not be so judgemental.

I say the above because I want to mention a feeling that cannot really stand the test of rationality. Here it goes: I feel that as we grow older, we are loved by a declining number of people. When I say love, I mean it in its demonstrative form: love as active care, love as demonstrated interest, love that is expressed and received.

I understand that this statement may not be mainstream. I also understand that it can be rationally refuted: as we grow older, we become more secure in our identities; people who know and socialise with us love us for who we truly are; and as our time on earth increases, so do our interactions with others, and with them our chances of meeting people and developing or deepening relationships of love.

Yet, I feel the opposite: that, as the years go by, the meaningful adult relationships in my life diminish in number and intensity, eroded by how busy and tired we all are. Eventually, if we are lucky, we are only deeply and meaningfully loved by our spouse, parents, and maybe siblings.

I acknowledge that the above is evidently unfair. I believe that my friends truly love me; that they would be genuinely devastated if something bad were to happen to me; and that they would show up without hesitation if I were in need.

What I feel and what I know to be the case are two different things, separated by an important nuance that perhaps sums up the loneliness of adulthood. Rational me knows that I am loved by many people. My close circle of friends dates back to my high school years; these are people with whom I grew up and with whom there is genuine and deep mutual care. Yet, as we grow older, the number of people for whom our well-being is of immediate concern is shrinking, even if their love for us does not.

Maybe the feeling that triggered these thoughts is not altogether wrong, only poorly articulated. While the main thesis–that we are loved by fewer people as we grow older–is evidently mistaken, put slightly differently, it holds some value: there are fewer people with the time, energy and inclination to remain such active participants in our lives that we experience their love every day. Although the love remains, it is revealed in moments of crisis rather than in the everyday.

There is nothing particularly wrong with that. I, too, do not have the time or energy to demonstrate my love on a daily basis to anyone who does not live in my house. This is one of the realities of adulthood, which is why adulthood and loneliness often go together, and why so many of our interactions become sporadic and centred on safer and less personal subjects, such as my beloved discussions about politics. So we are not loved by fewer people, but we live with less of that love.
